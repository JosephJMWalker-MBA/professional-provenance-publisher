Sherwin-Williams Technology & AI Leadership

Re: AI / R&D Scientific Integrity and Value Realization

Dear Sherwin-Williams Technology & AI Leadership,

I am reaching out to Sherwin-Williams, not merely responding to a requisition. My father spent his career with the company and retired from senior technology leadership, so Sherwin-Williams has been familiar to me for most of my life. Soon after college, I interviewed for a laboratory role there. The interviewer told me he thought I would eventually become bored in that position and would be better suited to corporate management. At the time, I interpreted the lack of a concrete next step as rejection. Looking back, I think he may have been identifying a role-fit problem rather than a company-fit problem.

Since then, I earned an MBA in Strategic Management, built a consulting practice, founded and operated technology ventures, published software, and designed production AI systems centered on provenance, auditability, deterministic controls, testing, and human judgment. I also remain genuinely comfortable in scientific environments: I enjoy laboratory work, precise protocols, accurate notes, and the discipline of allowing honest data to overrule a preferred conclusion.

Recent Sherwin-Williams postings make the intersection unusually concrete. The Director, AI Adoption and Value Realization role describes an enterprise AI operating model spanning the AI Center of Excellence, AI Technology, governance, risk, Finance, Legal, Cyber, and business value. Separately, the R&D Digital Knowledge Transformation Manager role describes organizing, cleansing, validating, standardizing, and structuring experimental findings and historical technical knowledge so they can support RAG, predictive modeling, AI-enabled retrieval, and broader digital transformation. That second workflow is especially close to the problem I have been thinking about.

The danger is not limited to a model hallucinating a fact. AI-assisted cleaning, labeling, schema mapping, summarization, classification, metadata inference, and hypothesis generation can subtly change the usable scientific dataset or cause an interpretation to acquire the apparent status of evidence. When machine-produced summaries and classifications feed back into later research notes and analyses, an organization can create a closed epistemic loop in which no single step looks fraudulent, yet the confidence of the final conclusion exceeds the evidence that actually exists.

At Sherwin-Williams' scale, a false signal is not merely a bad answer. It can consume scientist time, instrument capacity, raw materials, validation work, management attention, and capital while the organization chases a pattern partly manufactured by its own information architecture. The corresponding opportunity is to design AI-enabled R&D so that observed evidence, deterministic transformation, human interpretation, AI inference, hypothesis, and validation state remain explicitly distinct and auditable from the point of capture through executive decision-making.

That is the kind of system I would like to help Sherwin-Williams explore. A bounded first step could assess scientific-AI failure modes and current workflow assumptions, define provenance and evidence-status requirements, and prototype a governed architecture around a narrow use case. If the resulting capability creates durable advantage, Sherwin-Williams could then decide whether it belongs only inside the company, should be protected as intellectual property, or has external commercialization value where strategically appropriate.

I am not assuming from the outside that this problem is unsolved, nor am I asking Sherwin-Williams to force my background into a preexisting title. I would value the opportunity to pressure-test the thesis with the people already building the company's AI and R&D transformation capability. If the problem is real and the combination of scientific training, hands-on AI engineering, consulting, and business strategy is useful, I would welcome a conversation about the right structure for contributing—an internal role, a cross-functional innovation responsibility, or a scoped engagement.
